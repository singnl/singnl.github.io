Lego Rovers

Two Lego rover systems built for simulated Martian cargo delivery and disaster relief

Raspberry PiAutonomous SystemsSensorsPythonSLAMIterative Design

Two separate autonomous rover projects — MACRO and GEARS — each built for a different mission profile, sensor stack, and navigation strategy, developed across two semesters of iterative engineering design with cross-functional teams.

MACRO — Mars Cargo Rover

MACRO simulates autonomous cargo delivery across rugged Martian terrain for a fictional L3Harris/MITEER mission brief. The platform runs on a Raspberry Pi atop a Lego-bracket chassis, with a rear motorized ramp for cargo release and a front-mounted battery pack doing double duty as a counterweight — without it, the rover could only carry 142g before tipping; with it, capacity jumped to 1029g.

LOOP every cycle:
    IF obstacle within 15cm (ultrasonic):
        STOP
    ELSE IF magnetic beacon detected (IMU):
        increment beacon_count
        IF beacon_count == target_location:
            TURN toward drop-off, then release cargo
    ELSE:
        IF color_sensor == black:
            move forward
        ELSE:
            check left/right line finders → steer to re-center

Five sensors handle perceptual awareness: a front ultrasonic sensor triggers stops within 15 cm of an obstacle; dual IR line finders and a center-mounted color sensor keep the rover tracking a black line (including dashed sections) at controlled speed; and a front-mounted IMU counts magnetic sub-beacons along the track to identify which of three drop-off sites it has reached. Priority order — hazard detection, then magnet/cargo logic, then line-following — let higher-priority behaviors interrupt line tracking without losing the robot's place in the course.

Motor speed was calibrated empirically: five trials across five power settings produced a linear relationship between motor power and rover velocity, folded directly into the control code so a user could request a target speed in cm/s. Final testing held that target within 1.3% error. The team also pressure-tested the design against a scaled-up, full-size Mars deployment — modeling incline limits (24° prototype, scaled to a 40° target based on rover precedents like Opportunity's 32° slope climb), operating temperature ranges for the Pi and PLA housing, and battery runtime (2.86 hours prototype, ~3.28 hours projected on Mars with a larger NiMH pack).

At final demonstration, the speed-control and cargo drop-off subsystems performed as designed (drop-off landed within roughly six inches of target), but line-following struggled on a sharper turn than the team had tested for, preventing a full course completion — a result traced to conflicting logic between the line and color sensors, and used to inform recommended fixes (simplifying to one sensor system, adding stronger drive motors for inclines).

GEARS — Grand Emergency Aid Resource System

GEARS navigates a disaster-relief scenario to deliver humanitarian aid and map hazardous terrain for follow-up relief teams. The final chassis uses high-traction treads for zero-radius turning, driven by a Raspberry Pi, after an earlier wheel-and-swivel design was tested and set aside in favor of tighter in-place rotation.

FUNCTION moveForward():
    FOR i in 1 to 4:                  // 10cm increments, 40cm grid
        IF hazard detected (IR or IMU):
            moveBack(i increments)
            logHazard(position)
            treat hazard as wall → turn
            RETURN
        ELSE:
            advance 10cm
    updatePosition(direction, increment_count)

FUNCTION wallFollow():
    IF front wall detected:
        TURN right if no wall on right, else TURN left
    ELSE:
        adjust distance to right-side wall (bang-bang control)

Front and right-mounted ultrasonic sensors handle wall-following and turning logic; a front IR sensor detects heat sources (calibrated to a 120W threshold at 30 cm); and an under-chassis IMU flags magnetic field spikes — roughly 10x the ambient baseline — simulating damaged infrastructure like power stations. Rather than driving continuously, GEARS advances in precise 10 cm increments, taking a hazard reading at each step and logging its position to a coordinate grid. On mission completion, the full path — along with any mapped hazards — exports to a CSV file so future units could retrace a validated safe route.

The cargo container went through two CAD iterations: a gravity-sealed wedge-lid design and a hinged version for easier access. A weighted decision matrix (favoring lid stability, ease of opening, and manufacturability) selected the simpler gravity-sealed design, later refined with a pull-tab for easier opening. The deployment mechanism itself iterated from a rack-and-pinion slide (dropped due to gear misalignment) to a swiveling arm that proved more reliable in testing.

Cultural and ethical design work included a symbol system for labeling cargo contents (medical supplies, food/water, shelter, fuel) — developed through brainstorming, a 10-respondent local survey, and weighted decision matrices scoring cultural familiarity, importance, and universality, landing on icons like a caduceus-with-herbs for medical supplies and a flame-and-gas-pump combination for fuel. The team also reasoned through color psychology and chassis shape (rounded edges, earthy packaging tones vs. a tread-based chassis that could visually read as military in conflict zones) to keep the system approachable to at-risk communities.

Final demonstration results: 13-minute mission time (against an 18-minute hard limit and a 12-minute stretch goal), 4 cm cargo placement accuracy, at least 10 cm of hazard clearance maintained throughout, and 100% hazard-mapping and symbol-recognition accuracy. Wall-following showed some oscillation traced to bang-bang control (a smoother PID approach was recommended for future revisions), and battery drain over the run caused some cargo drop-off inconsistency — both flagged as priority fixes going forward.
